Overall, I've been making music for ~25 years or so now; in terms of performing, I fiddled with Ableton, grooveboxes,
and even tried [livecoding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qrEFcg8eyq4). But I was never too interested in DJing until a couple of years ago,
when I decided to give it a go, with various breaks here and there.

Recently I picked it up again, got myself a new DJ controller, downloaded ~30 of
my favourite tunes in my target genre, connected everything, loaded [Traktor](https://www.native-instruments.com/pages/traktor) and started playing.

Long story short, I ended up building a small Haskell program to help me plan DJ sets.
This post explains where the idea came from and how the problem can be described simply using graphs.
It also gives an overview of how the source code is organized and how its main parts relate to the theoretical concepts discussed here.
The full code is available [on GitHub](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy).


## Where the idea came from

For those unfamiliar with it, DJing is basically about _transitioning smoothly_ from one song to the
next using volume changes, EQ, effects, etc.

But obviously not all songs blend well together, so transitions can be tricky.
To make a smooth transition between song A and song B, usually a few things need to happen:

 * the songs have a similar tempo
 * they have a compatible musical key  (more on this later)
 * they're "in sync" (meaning they play on time) and lined up in a way that "makes sense" (what we call _phrasing_ - outside the scope of this post)

Back in the old days, "real DJs" knew their entire music library
by heart, even if it filled several heavy bags of vinyl records. They knew exactly which songs
blended well together and which didn't.

Nowadays, the heavy bags are replaced by USB sticks full of MP3s
and we have software tools to help decide which track to play next.

One such tool is what we call _harmonic mixing_ (see e.g. [here](https://mixedinkey.com/harmonic-mixing-guide/)), which tries to solve the problem of choosing
tracks that have compatible musical keys.

How does it work in practice?
The DJ software analyzes each song and assigns it one of 24 musical keys, labelled
with a number from 1 to 12 and a letter (usually `d` or `m`, e.g. in Traktor, or `A` or `B` in other systems).
Here, for example, the track has been assigned `11d`:

![Okinawa!!](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/df-okinawa.png){: width="600" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

So what can we do with this information?
That's where the _Camelot wheel_ comes in handy (see detailed info and interactive example [here](https://mixedinkey.com/camelot-wheel/)).
Basically, once we know the key of a song, the wheel suggests a set of tonally compatible keys:

![Camelot wheel](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/camelot-2.png){: width="400" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

For example, from 3A we can go "horizontally" to 2A and 4A or "vertically" to 3B (or stay in 3A obviously).

With our song above (`11d` in Traktor), the same rules let us go to `12d`, `10d`, `11m` or just stay in `11d`.

In general, potentially compatible songs have either:

 * the same key
 * a number one higher or lower (wrapping around between 12 and 1), with the same letter
 * the same number but a "flipped" letter (a "vertical" jump to the relative minor or major key, if you're curious)

In our program, this is formalized in [`Camelot.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Domain/Camelot.hs)
(we use Traktor's Open Key notation, which uses `d` for major and `m` for minor and numbers the wheel differently, but the idea is the same).

In general, however, as music people often say, music theory is just a guideline; it can definitely
help us make informed decisions or demystify certain phenomena (like why certain chord sequences always seem to sound so good),
but ultimately our ears are the final judge: "if it sounds good, it _is_ good".

In fact, while using this system to mix songs from my folder I quickly discovered that...

  * not all compatible songs necessarily sounded great when overlapped
  * some songs, although officially incompatible, actually sounded great together and I really loved the transition

So I started writing down my discoveries on a notebook page:

```
Song A --> Song B (OMG really loved this!!)
Song X --> Song Y (Try blending the vocal, sounds great)
Song B --> Song X
...
```

and referring to it as I was mixing, alongside Traktor's harmonic mixing suggestions.

Eventually, I was using harmonic mixing as a guide for exploration, while also taking the occasional risk
and writing down my discoveries, ending up with something like this:

![Camelot wheel](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/notebook.png){: width="600" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

That's when I realized that I was essentially just building a graph which could, in theory, be used in some
interesting ways not only to decide what to play next, but also to build full playlists and whatnot.

But there was a problem: this was already getting painful to manage. Once I had two pages I had to keep flipping
between them; what would happen when I reached 10?

So I decided to build a program to handle all this.
During that same period I was also (re)learning Haskell
so I thought great, let's do it in Haskell.
If nothing else, it could be a chance to learn something beyond the usual textbook exercises.

## So, this is just a graph problem...

Yes, those notebook entries naturally describe a graph of what we will call _tested transitions_:
transitions between songs that the user has personally tried, loved and decided were worth recording for next time.

The graph looks something like this:

![Camelot wheel](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/simple-graph.png){: width="400" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

Here, track `t1` transitions nicely into `t2` and `t4`, which both transition nicely into `t3`.
`t5` is sadly left out (solution: add more music to the library, explore and add tested transitions once discovered!).

So those are our _tested transitions_, which we explicitly keep track of.
But, as we saw, we can also use the Camelot wheel to suggest potentially good tracks to play next.

How can we combine the two approaches?

Here, each segment of the Camelot wheel contains songs (the black dots),
and the arrows represent our tested transitions.


![Camelot wheel](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/camelot-1.png){: width="500" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

In other words, given a set of songs, the Camelot wheel gives us a _partition_ of that set into 24 groups.
The harmonic mixing rules then give us _implicit_ transitions which we could use alongside the _explicit_ (or _tested_) transitions.

In summary, given a song we could follow:

 * the implicit edges given by the Camelot wheel
 * explicit edges recorded by the user

You should now be able to start seeing how we could, in principle, ask interesting questions such as

 * give me 5 possible next songs to play after song X
 * give me a playlist of 10 songs to play (that sound good with each other), starting from X
 * give me a playlist of 10 songs to play starting from a random one
 * give me a playlist of 10 songs that must include X, Y and Z and end with T
 * ...


## Formalizing the problem

To summarize all of the above, the data structure we end up with looks something like this:

![Camelot wheel](/assets/pics/dj-buddy-post/graph.png){: width="800" style="display: block; margin-left: auto; margin-right: auto;"}

Here, black dots are songs and solid arrows are tested transitions.
Songs are grouped by key into 24 partitions, shown as coloured circles.

Dashed arrows represent transitions suggested by harmonic mixing (only a
subset is shown, but hopefully you get the idea).

In this example we can see that from `9A` we can get to `8A` and `10A` as well as `9B` and `9A` itself.
Tested transitions can also break the harmonic mixing rules, like `t4` to `t9` here, which is totally fine.

You might have noticed numbers on the transitions. Those numbers represent the _cost_ of each transition.

For example, a tested transition costs zero, because the user has personally tried it and loves it.
Transitioning to a song with the same key costs 1, while moving to a compatible (but different) key costs 2.
The idea is that a shared key is a safer bet, while a compatible key is a bit more risky.
Neither guarantees a great transition, as we've seen (remember, our ears are the judge).

Picking a random song in an incompatible key would, in theory, carry a much higher cost.
But if the user has tested that transition and loves it, it still costs zero:
"tested transition always wins" - our version of "if it sounds good it is good".

All of this is formalized in Haskell in [`MusicLibrary.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Domain/MusicLibrary.hs) and [`Transitions.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Domain/Transitions.hs).

## OK so how does it work in practice?

If you've followed this far, the rest should be fairly simple.

Given a starting song, we can just follow outgoing edges to figure out which tracks to play next;
if there are any tested transitions (cost = 0) then great, we can pick one of those.
Otherwise, we follow the rules of harmonic mixing, picking a track with the same (cost = 1) or a compatible key (cost = 2).

We use _transition costs_ to help us pick the "best" transition at any given time.

Building an entire playlist or DJ set is just an extension of this where we repeat the process for a
given number of steps (or until we run out of tracks to play) - see [`Playlist.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Domain/Playlist.hs).

As the user adds new songs to their library and continues using the system, they might eventually discover new
transitions (guided by harmonic mixing and a bit of chaos)
and add them to the graph, which in turn "unlocks" interesting new combinations and potential playlists.

## How is the project built?

The project is open source and available [here](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/tree/main).

It currently has 3 main parts:

* a library of pure functions for modelling the graph, recommending tracks and building playlists. In turn, it has two components:
  * a small set of functions that clients (CLI, web app etc.) can call directly (see [`App.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/App.hs)).
  * a bigger set of internal functions that provide the building blocks (see the [`Domain`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/tree/main/src/Domain) folder).
* a [CLI application](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/app/Main.hs) that allows (adventurous) DJs to use the program from the terminal. This is built with
[optparse-applicative](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/optparse-applicative).
* a [small web app](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/web/Main.hs) for the (slightly less adventurous) DJ to use from a browser. This one is built with [Scotty](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/scotty).

The idea was to keep both the CLI and web server minimal - basically all they do is call into [`App.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/App.hs),
get their data and present it to the user.

The music library currently lives in a simple YAML file, handwritten by the user
(consistent with the manual curation ethos of the project).

```yaml
tracks:
  - id: first_track
    title: First Track
    key: "3D"
  - id: second_track
    title: Second Track
    key: "4D"
transitions:
  - from: first_track
    to: second_track
    notes: "Mix after the breakdown" # optional
```

The YAML file is read and parsed into a [`MusicLibrary`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Domain/MusicLibrary.hs#L65), the main data structure handled by
our program (see [`Yaml.hs`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/Persistence/Yaml.hs) for details about the parsing,
for which we used [aeson](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/aeson)).

The program currently treats the library as _read-only_: the user maintains it, the program reads it
and does its magic. Adding or removing songs and transitions through the program is left for
future work (see below), along with importing libraries from DJ software such as Traktor, Serato or rekordbox.

Throughout the program we handle errors via `Either AppError _`, where [`AppError`](https://github.com/dfilaretti/dj-buddy/blob/main/src/App.hs#L45)
represents the various things that can go wrong.
This gives the CLI and web app details about what happened, so they can show meaningful error messages.

## Future work

Ultimately, this all reduces to graph problems; the fact that we have "songs" and "transitions" is just a detail.

### Ability to add more constraints in playlist generation

At the moment we can ask "give me a 10-track playlist starting from song X".
One cool addition would be "give me a 10-track playlist starting from X, making sure you
include Y and Z and end in T". Same idea, but with constraints.
This probably maps to some existing graph algorithm but we haven't looked into it yet.

### Looking further ahead than just the next song

At the moment, the algorithm only looks at the next step: given song X, it looks at the candidates
and picks the one with the lowest cost (choosing randomly if there's more than one with that same cost).

This seems to work fine in practice, but isn't always ideal.

Suppose we're playing song X and find a tested transition to song Y - great!
It sounds amazing, but unfortunately Y turns out to be a dead end, as now we don't have any compatible
songs to choose from...

Instead, we could have chosen another song Z: perhaps, going from X to Z wouldn't be as amazing as going from X to Y,
but once we reached Z, we'd have many more transitions at our disposal, helping us avoid getting stuck and
allowing the DJ set to continue smoothly (perhaps even unlocking interesting new areas of the library).

### Library imports from established DJ software

The YAML file is currently maintained by hand. Importing track metadata from Traktor, rekordbox, and similar tools would make larger libraries easier to manage while keeping transition curation manual.

### Richer metadata

The format is deliberately minimal. BPM is omitted because my sets stay around 138–140 BPM, so I already assume the tracks are tempo-compatible. Artist names are also omitted because I know this small collection well. Both would be useful additions as the library grows, but add little to the current proof of concept.

### Library management via the software

As mentioned above, the library is currently curated manually by the user.
But this can become cumbersome as the library grows.
It would be nice to manage songs and transitions without editing the YAML.
Also, in a web setting this might have the potential to look very cool (see below).

### Fancy Web UI

Yes, this is a graph, and we _could_ build something really cool looking that runs in a
web browser, letting users visualize and interact with the graph etc... Pretty cool but out of scope.

## FAQs

### So what's the practical use of this? Is this better than harmonic mixing?

The two things are not in competition.
Harmonic mixing gives us starting points to experiment and explore.
When the user finds something really exciting, they can record it.
It's a personal curation system.
Harmonic mixing and the various AI song analysis systems tell us what _might_ sound good; this tells us
what _sounds freaking amazing_ because the user has personally tested it.

### Is this AI? Is there any plan to integrate AI?

No. In fact, the idea here is just to build a sort of database of personally curated transitions.
There's already plenty of software that suggests tracks. This is for users to curate
their own library of amazing transitions.
